What this episode actually teaches

Words like amazing, love and “make no mistakes” say how much you want it, and the model can’t check any of them. A good prompt says who it’s for, what it does, what not to do, what you want back, and when to stop and ask. Leave one out and the model fills the gap with a guess. A question costs seconds; a guess can cost the afternoon.

What this episode actually teaches

That vibe coding is not the problem and the model is not the problem — the prompt is. AJ wins on four clauses any viewer can copy: who opens it, what is normally on it, what happens when it is empty, and what not to touch. None of that is technique. It is saying out loud the things you already know and assume the machine does too.

Two things to hold while drawing it

Peter’s page has to genuinely look better in panel 12. If the audience does not want Peter’s version, panel 14 is not a reversal, it is a told-you-so. And the AI never gloats — panel 17 is the only line that states the lesson, it is flat, and it credits AJ rather than condemning Peter.

01

The premise

Someone uses a word. You nod, because asking costs status — and the more senior you are, the more it costs. So you ask the machine instead, privately.

Every episode is self-contained. Nothing needs explaining beforehand, no prior episode is required, and nothing below this line needs to be understood by a viewer. Start with the script.

02

Three buckets, three outros

Every episode is filed under speed, cost or quality — the three things the product works on. Nobody is ever told this. It decides one thing: which of three fixed endings the episode gets. That is the whole brand connection, and it lives in the shape of the series rather than in anyone's dialogue.

Cost

“…hey. What happened to my AI credits?”

Credits remaining: 0
Speed

“…hey. What time is it?”

a clock, at an absurd hour
Quality

“…hey. How many files did it change?”

1,847 files changed · 0 reviewed

Same staging every time: the stamp holds, one small lit thing appears under it, and the episode's protagonist — whoever it belonged to — asks one flat question. The answer is always a number that is obviously wrong. That is why “credits” works and a vaguer noun wouldn't.

A term gets its bucket from where the joke pays off, not from what the term technically is. Nearly everything touches all three — speed / cost / quality is the fast-cheap-good triangle. context window would be quality if the punchline were forgetting, and cost if it's paying for a night of nothing. The ending picks the bucket, so you have to know your ending before you commit.
